SALES FORECASTING · INVENTORY DECISIONS

A better forecast.
A better order?

I built a Python workflow to test whether forecasting and optimization improve inventory decisions over simple ordering rules.

42 product–store series · 17 historical episodes · 4 policies
01 / PROBLEM

One budget. Competing products.

Order too little and sales go unfilled. Order too much and stock remains. How should a retailer allocate a limited purchasing budget when future sales are uncertain?

02 / COMPARISON

Test the decision, not just the prediction.

Compare trailing-average orders, forecast-based orders, point-forecast optimization and scenario optimization using the same budget, initial stock and delivery assumptions within each episode.

03 / FINDING

Complexity did not consistently win.

−0.51%point optimization cost*
8 / 17lower-cost windows*
+3.19%scenario optimization cost*

*Compared with trailing-average orders. Point optimization had the lowest total modeled cost, but trailing-average orders had the higher unit fill rate: 79.87% vs 79.35%.

04 / RECOMMENDATION

Keep the benchmark. Require stronger evidence.

The small aggregate cost advantage is insufficient for an operational recommendation. Retain trailing-average ordering as the practical benchmark and validate the point optimizer prospectively before adoption.

The analytical lesson

Better average prediction does not guarantee better allocation. The useful output is a defensible policy choice—including when the evidence does not justify a more complex method.

What I implemented

Data validation and provenance → time-ordered forecasts → budget-constrained integer orders → historical policy replay → Streamlit decision review. Independent saved-order accounting checks covered all 68 policy-window results.

Research prototype. Exploratory retrospective evaluation, not a fresh holdout. Operating costs and stock are assumed; observed sales may understate demand. Episodes reset initial inventory. No actual retailer savings, statistical significance or production readiness is claimed. V1 and the follow-up use different product selections and must not be presented as a controlled performance improvement.

Source: official M5 data. No real orders are placed. Decision review is deterministic; no LLM computes quantities. The app runs locally; this page presents the evidence.
